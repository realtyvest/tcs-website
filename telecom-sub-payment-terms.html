How Telecom Subs Actually Get Paid, and the Part You Can Control

ByGil Ramirez
PublishedOctober 12, 2026
Read Time6 minutes

The short answer: telecom subs get paid on the customer's clock: the carrier's or prime's terms, after the invoice is approved, often through a vendor portal. Pay clauses and retainage can push that clock out further. You can't change their clock. You can control how fast a finished job turns into a clean invoice.

This page is general information, not legal advice. Read your subcontract and ask a construction attorney about your own terms.

The Three Clocks on Every Dollar

  • Your clock: work done to invoice sent.
  • Their approval: invoice sent to invoice approved.
  • Their terms: invoice approved to paid.

Only the first clock is yours.

Carrier and Prime Payment Terms

Terms are set by the customer. Carrier terms can run long. On carrier and prime work, payment usually starts counting after the invoice is approved, not after you submit it, and approval often runs through a vendor portal.

Pay-When-Paid vs Pay-If-Paid

A pay-when-paid clause governs timing. As the American Bar Association puts it, "the subcontractor will be paid within some fixed time period after the contractor itself is paid." A pay-if-paid clause makes the owner's payment a condition of yours, which shifts the risk of nonpayment down to the sub.

Under the majority view, pay-if-paid is a condition precedent and pay-when-paid is a timing provision (Smith, Gambrell & Russell). Enforceability of pay-if-paid varies widely by jurisdiction, while pay-when-paid is generally treated as a timing tool, with payment due within a reasonable time (ABA Forum on Construction Law).

Retainage: the Money Held Until Closeout

Retainage is money held back from each payment until closeout. Many states cap it, often at 5 or 10 percent, and some leave it to your contract. Pay clauses can delay the release of retainage too (ConsensusDocs). A late or incomplete closeout package keeps it held longer, so the fiber closeout package checklist is worth running before the last invoice.

What You Can Control: Days to Invoice

Days to Invoice is the number of days from work complete to invoice sent. Every day there is added in front of the customer's clock.

  • Close out production at the end of each day, while the crew still remembers it.
  • Match the invoice to the customer's units and codes.
  • Send the invoice at the cadence the contract allows.
  • Track why invoices get kicked back, and fix the top causes.

Before You Sign the Next Subcontract

  • What triggers payment: submission, approval, or the prime getting paid.
  • The exact pay clause wording.
  • The retainage percent and what releases it.
  • Invoice cadence and format.
  • Portal and approval steps.
  • Closeout requirements.

Questions Contractors Ask

How do telecom subcontractors get paid?

On the customer's terms. The carrier, prime or operator approves the invoice, often through a vendor portal, and pays on its own schedule. Pay clauses in your subcontract and retainage can add more time.

What is the difference between pay-when-paid and pay-if-paid?

Pay-when-paid is generally about timing: the prime pays you within a set time after it gets paid. Pay-if-paid makes the owner's payment a condition, which can move the risk of nonpayment onto you. How courts treat each one depends on your state.

When is retainage released?

Usually at closeout, under the terms of your contract and any state law that applies. Many states cap it, often at 5 or 10 percent, and some leave it to your contract. An incomplete closeout package can keep it held longer.

Can I make a carrier or prime pay faster?

Usually not. Their terms are set by contract. What you can control is how fast you turn finished work into a clean, complete invoice, so their clock starts sooner and fewer invoices get kicked back.

Are pay-if-paid clauses legal?

It depends on the state. Some states enforce clear pay-if-paid clauses, some limit them, and some ban them. This is general information, not legal advice. Have a construction attorney review your subcontract.
