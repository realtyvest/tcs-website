31,000 BEAD Locations Just Opened Up in Texas

By Gil Ramirez August 16, 2026 5 min read

Winning bidders in Texas just walked away from 31,000 BEAD locations. If you work in Texas or want to, this is the most important shift in the state's broadband buildout this year.

BEAD stands for Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment. It is the $42.5 billion federal program running through each state's broadband office to connect unserved and underserved locations. In Texas, the program is administered by the Comptroller's office. Winning bidders are the ISPs and providers who submitted bids and were awarded specific location sets. When they back out, those locations go back into play.

What "Back Out" Actually Means for Your Pipeline

A winning bidder backing out is not a minor paperwork issue. That ISP agreed to serve those locations, won the award, and then declined to move forward. Texas now has to figure out what happens to 31,000 homes and businesses that were supposed to be covered.

The state has a few options. It can rebid those locations, reassign them, or in some cases fold them into other award areas. What it cannot do is ignore them. Federal rules require BEAD funds to cover the locations they were earmarked for.

This is the question on your desk this week: do you have a relationship with an ISP that could absorb some of these locations, or are you on the outside watching?

The Amazon Angle Worth Watching

The excerpt from Broadband Breakfast notes that Texas was optimistic it would finalize a contract with Amazon, which had refused its award in Nebraska. The scraped article body was not available, so additional detail on the Amazon situation is not confirmed here. What is confirmed: Amazon has already walked away from a BEAD award in at least one state. Texas was trying to bring them in anyway.

That tells you something. States are scrambling to fill gaps left by bidders who calculated the numbers and decided the deal did not work. Whether that is because construction costs came in higher than bid, labor was not available, or the service territory was too remote to pencil out, the result is the same. Locations without a builder.

Every telecom contractor in Texas should be paying attention to who is actually positioned to do this work.

What You Should Do Before This Resets

When locations go back out for rebid, the ISP that picks them up will need boots on the ground fast. That ISP is going to call contractors it already trusts. Here is the practical list:

  • Get in front of ISPs operating in the affected Texas counties before the rebid process closes. The broadband office will publish updated award information. Watch for it.
  • Know your capacity. If an ISP calls you with 500 locations in a compressed window, what is your honest answer? Have that number ready.
  • Review your bonding and insurance. BEAD subgrantees, meaning the ISPs receiving state funds, have to pass federal compliance requirements down to the contractors they use. Your paperwork needs to be current.
  • Ask your ISP contacts directly: are they affected by any of these vacated awards? Some ISPs picked up additional location sets they may not have planned to build themselves.

The Bigger Pattern Here

Texas is not alone. Nebraska saw an Amazon walkaway. Other states are dealing with bidders who are quietly reassessing whether their original numbers hold up against actual construction costs in 2026. Materials are not cheaper. Qualified crews are not more available. Fuel is not free.

The contractors who stay close to their ISP contacts and stay financially ready, meaning clean AR, working capital in place, bonding capacity available, are the ones who get the call when a location set drops back into play. The ones who are heads-down and out of contact miss the window entirely.

I have seen this play out more than once. The rebid moves faster than the first round. The ISP is already behind schedule. They do not have time to vet a new contractor from scratch.

Be the contractor they already know.

When a state has to rebid 31,000 locations, the first calls go to contractors who stayed in the room.
