Fiber-First Framework: What It Means for Your Next Bid

By Gil Ramirez August 27, 2026 5 min read

The Fiber Broadband Association published a paper in May 2026 pushing a fiber-first framework for U.S. broadband policy. That framework is not academic. It shapes which projects get funded, which technologies qualify, and where your next three years of work come from.

Why a Trade Group Paper Moves Money

The FBA is not a regulatory body. It does not write law. But its position papers land on desks at NTIA (the National Telecommunications and Information Administration, the federal agency running BEAD) and in state broadband offices that are still finalizing their deployment rules.

When a well-funded trade group publishes a framework that says fiber should be the default technology for federally funded broadband, state program managers pay attention. Some states have already written fiber preference into their BEAD initial proposals. Others are still deciding.

This paper adds political weight to the fiber column at exactly the moment states are closing their subgrantee selection processes. Subgrantees are the ISPs and utilities that win state BEAD contracts and then hire telecom contractors to do the build.

The Bid Pipeline Question Sitting on Your Desk

Here is the call you have to make right now.

If your crews run fiber, the FBA framework is a tailwind. ISPs pitching fiber-first projects have a stronger policy argument when competing for state BEAD dollars. That means more fiber build contracts flowing to subgrantees, and more subgrantee work flowing to you.

If your crews run coax or fixed wireless support work, watch which states your ISP customers are bidding in. A state that adopts a strict fiber-first rule will disadvantage those ISPs in the BEAD process. That does not mean those ISPs go away. It means their BEAD volume shrinks and your pipeline from that customer shrinks with it.

The FBA paper's full research detail was not available in the source material reviewed for this article. The specific dollar figures, technology thresholds, and policy recommendations in the paper have not been independently reviewed here. What is documented is the FBA's stated intent: fiber as the default for long-term U.S. connectivity investment.

What Telecom Contractors Should Do Before the Next RFP

Three things are worth doing this week.

  • Check your state's BEAD status. As of August 2026, most states are in subgrantee selection or early deployment phases. Your state broadband office website will show where they stand. If your state has a fiber preference written into its rules, your fiber-capable crews are already in demand.
  • Ask your ISP customers directly. Which BEAD awards are they pursuing? Which technology did they propose? If they proposed fiber and win, you want to be the contractor they call. If they proposed fixed wireless, know that their BEAD volume may be limited in fiber-first states.
  • Price your fiber capacity honestly. BEAD projects run on tight margins and tight timelines. If you have three fiber crews and someone wants to give you work for ten, that is a working capital problem waiting to happen. Know your crew margin before you commit.

The Longer Game Here

Federal broadband policy has shifted fast over the past four years. The FBA's fiber-first framework is one input, not the final word. Congress, NTIA, and individual state legislatures all retain influence over how BEAD money moves and what technologies qualify.

But the direction is clear. Fiber is where the long-term federal investment is pointing. Contractors who build fiber capacity now, in crews, equipment, and bonding capacity, will be positioned when the volume hits. Contractors who wait will be hiring into a tight labor market against competitors who started two years ago.

I have seen this play out in state-level programs before BEAD. The contractors who captured the most work were not always the biggest. They were the ones who knew which ISPs were winning, got to those ISPs early, and had the paperwork ready before the RFP dropped.
