Telecom RFPs: How to Read One, Decide to Bid, and Keep Track of It

RFPs and bid requests arrive by email, portal, and phone call. Miss a due date and the work goes to somebody else. Win the work and lose track of what was authorized, and the invoice gets questioned. A simple tracking habit covers both.

Short answer: A telecom RFP is a request from an ISP, prime or public agency asking contractors to price a defined scope of work. It lists the work, the units and the date the bid is due. You read it, price it in their terms, and send it in on time.

What a Telecom RFP Is

A telecom RFP is a request for proposal. An ISP, prime contractor, utility, municipality, or BEAD subgrantee sends it to ask contractors to price a defined scope of work, such as fiber placement, splicing, or drops, by a set due date.

You will also see RFQs and bid packages. An RFQ usually asks for prices on a scope that is already fixed. A bid package is the set of documents a customer sends with the request. The names vary by customer, but the job is the same: read what they want, price it in their terms, and get it in on time.

What Is Inside a Telecom RFP

Expect the scope and maps, the rate codes and units (feet for cable and conduit, each for drops, splices, and locates), the schedule and due dates, and the insurance and documents the customer requires.

Read the documentation standards closely: photos, test results, and as-builts. The closeout package checklist shows what that paperwork looks like at the end of a job. Read the payment terms carefully too, since they decide how long you carry the cost of the work.

How to Read a Unit-Price Sheet: A Worked Example

Fiber bids are often priced by the unit. The price sheet (also called a bid form or bid tab) lists each kind of work on its own line. Here is how to read one, line by line.

Example only. The lines below are made up for this guide. They do not come from a real bid. Prices are left blank on purpose: your prices come from your own costs.

LineWorkUnitEst. qtyYour unit priceLine total
1Directional bore, 2 inch conduitLF (feet)4,200___qty x price
2Place 144-count fiber in conduitLF4,500___qty x price
3Handhole, 17 x 30 inch, set and gradeEA (each)9___qty x price
4Splice closure, place and spliceEA3___qty x price
5Rock adder, bore in rockLF300___qty x price
6Restore paved surfaceSF (square feet)250___qty x price
7OTDR test, both directionsEA (per fiber)144___qty x price

Read it this way:

  1. Read the unit first. Feet, each and square feet are not the same. Price each line in its own unit. The fiber line (2) has more feet than the bore (1). That is common: slack and runs into handholes add cable.
  2. The quantity is an estimate. "Est. qty" is the owner's estimate. Find out if you get paid on the estimate or on what you actually build. Unit-price work often pays on what you build, but check the bid's payment terms.
  3. Line total is quantity times unit price. Add the line totals for your bid total. Some bid forms say the unit price wins if the math does not match. Check yours, and check your math.
  4. Find what each line includes. Is the conduit in line 1 yours to buy, or does the owner supply it? Is the 811 locate part of the bore, or its own line? If the sheet does not say, ask before the questions deadline.
  5. Price the adders. Lines like 5 and 6 only pay if the work happens. Do not hide rock or paving cost inside the bore price, or you can lose the bid on a job with none.
  6. Price the paperwork. Testing (line 7), photos and as-builts take crew time. If the sheet has no line for them, they live inside your other prices.
  7. Match the codes to your price book. Write down which of your codes fits each line. After the award, these lines usually set what you can bill. Check your contract. See "After the Award: Authorized Quantities" below.

The test: could someone in your office read your priced sheet and know what each line covers, without calling the estimator? If not, write your assumptions on the bid.

Where to Find Telecom and Fiber Bids

Bids do not come from one place. Several of these places are public and free to search. Here is where to look, and what each place is good for.

State broadband offices. Each state and territory has a broadband office that runs its BEAD program. NTIA has approved all 56 BEAD final proposals (NTIA, Aug 25, 2026). Start with your state's BEAD page. Some states also run a contractor directory, like Minnesota. Get listed where you can.

BEAD subgrantee lists. The state does not hire your crew. The ISPs that won subgrants do. Some state broadband offices post the winners as a file you can download, called "Exhibit A, Subgrantees" (for example, Mississippi and Arkansas). That file is your call list. Get on each ISP's vendor list before it starts to build. The BEAD subcontractor guide covers what they ask for.

SAM.gov. This is where federal agencies post their bids. Anyone can search it for free at sam.gov/opportunities, with no account. Search by NAICS code 237130 (Power and Communication Line and Related Structures Construction) and by the states you can reach. With a free Login.gov account, you can save searches and follow changes to the bids you care about (SAM.gov).

School and library fiber bids (E-Rate). Schools and libraries that want E-Rate money must post a bid request, FCC Form 470, for at least 28 days. Some post an RFP with it, such as when state or local bid rules call for one. You can search and download these for free with USAC's Form 470 Download Tool (USAC).

Utility and co-op bid pages. Electric co-ops, power utilities and public utilities often post fiber and make-ready bids on their own procurement pages. Check the ones in your area every week, and register as a vendor where they let you.

State, city and county bid portals. State offices, DOTs, counties and cities post fiber and conduit work on their own bid pages. For example, New York's Empire State Development posts its open bids, broadband builds too, on its Requests for Proposals page. Sign up as a vendor so the notices come to you.

ISP and prime vendor portals. Once you are on an ISP's or a prime's vendor list, its bids come to you by email or in its portal. This is where repeat work lives.

How to keep your own live bid list

No one list has every bid. So build your own, and keep it current:

  1. Save a SAM.gov search and follow the bids that fit.
  2. Check the Form 470 tool for your states.
  3. Download your state's subgrantee file. Add each ISP to your call list.
  4. Put each utility, co-op and city bid page on a weekly check.
  5. Log every bid the day it shows up, in one place.

That last step is the one that keeps a bid from slipping past you. Many contractors watch more than one of these places at once. That is why a single log matters more than any one source.

Deciding Whether to Bid

Before you price it, check that you have crews free in the window, that the codes fit your price book, and that you can meet the insurance limits and documents the customer asks for. Paperwork standards count too: photos, test results, and as-builts all take crew time, so price them into the unit rates. Customer documents are covered in contractor compliance tracking.

How to Answer a Telecom RFP

Send your questions by the questions due date. Price the work in the RFP's own codes and units, not your internal names. List your assumptions and exclusions so the customer knows what the price covers. Submit before the bid due date, then log it.

What to Log for Every RFP

Who and where

The customer, the project, the location, and where the request came from: the portal, the contact, or the email thread.

Dates

Questions due, bid due, expected award, and expected start. The bid due date is the one that loses work when it is missed.

Scope

The rate codes and quantities in the RFP's own units: feet for cable and conduit, each for drops, splices, and locates.

Status and decision

Reviewing, bidding, submitted, won, lost, or no bid, with the reason for the decision.

How to Track Telecom RFPs Step by Step

You can do all of this in a plain spreadsheet. Use one row for each RFP.

  1. Log it the day it comes in. Add a row to your sheet the day the RFP shows up. Note who sent it and where it came from.
  2. Write down the key dates. Put in the due date, the last day to ask questions, and the walk date if there is one.
  3. Copy the codes and units. Use the RFP's own codes and units. Feet stay feet. Each stays each.
  4. Set a go or no-go date. Pick a day to decide if you will bid. Write the reason down too.
  5. Log the bid you sent. Fill in the date you sent the bid and the amount you bid.
  6. Log the award. When you win, write down the award and the work they said you can do and bill.

After the Award: Authorized Quantities

The awarded RFP or PO sets what you are authorized to bill. Keep the authorized codes and quantities where the office can see them for the life of the job.

Compare the tally to the authorized quantities before you send the invoice. If the crew did more than was authorized, get the change order approved before you bill it, not after.

How Telecom Procurement Requests Get Approved

Each customer has its own way to approve work. The path often looks like this.

First, the customer sets the scope. Next, a team reviews the bids that come in. Then the customer picks a bid and makes the award. Last, it can send a notice to proceed. That is the go to start work.

Steps can change by customer and by job. Read the RFP to see how this one works. If you are not sure, ask the customer before the questions deadline.

What Has to Be True for It to Work

Use the RFP's codes

If your price book uses different names, map them once when you log the award, not on every invoice.

Capture quantities in the RFP's units

Feet for cable and conduit, each for drops, splices, and locates.

Change orders update what is authorized

An approved change order adds to the authorized quantities. Keep it with the job, not in an email thread.

One person owns the log

The log is only as current as the person who updates it. Update it the day an RFP arrives.

Questions contractors ask

What is an RFP in telecom?

A request for proposal from an ISP, prime contractor, utility, or public agency asking contractors to price a defined scope of telecom work, such as fiber placement, splicing, or drops, by a set due date. It usually lists the rate codes, quantities, schedule, insurance, and documentation the customer expects.

What is the simplest way to start tracking RFPs?

One shared log with a row per RFP: customer, project, due date, scope, status, and the decision. A spreadsheet works if one person owns it and it is updated the day an RFP arrives.

What happens when the crew does more than the RFP authorized?

Compare the tally to the authorized quantities before you send the invoice. If the work went over, get a change order approved first, or bill to the authorized quantity and note the difference.

Should we track the bids we lose?

Yes. Log the result and the reason if the customer gives one. Over time the log shows which customers, regions, and job types you win, and where your bids miss.

What is the difference between an RFP, RFQ and RFI in telecom?

An RFP asks you to plan and price the work. An RFQ usually asks for a price on work that is already defined. An RFI asks for facts about your crew and what you can do; it is usually not a bid.

Where do fiber contractors find telecom RFPs?

Look at state broadband office sites and BEAD subgrantee lists. Check ISP vendor portals and public bid boards too. Where alerts are offered, sign up so new ones come to you.

How long does a telecom RFP response usually take?

It depends on the scope and the dates the RFP sets. Check the due date and the questions deadline in the RFP itself.

Can BEAD subcontractors respond to RFPs directly?

It depends on the RFP and your state's BEAD rules. Some RFPs are open to subs and some go only through the subgrantee. Read who may respond in the RFP. If it's unclear, ask the issuer before the questions deadline.

What is an RFP tracker?

An RFP tracker is one shared log with a row for each RFP. It shows who sent it, the project, the due date, the scope, the status and the decision. One person owns it and updates it the day an RFP lands. After the award, it also holds the codes and quantities the customer authorized.

How do I read the price sheet in a telecom RFP?

Read each line left to right: the work, the unit (feet or each), the quantity, and your unit price. Unit price times quantity is the line total. Then check what each line covers. Is material in or out? Are rock, pavement or extra conduit on their own lines? Price every line the RFP asks for.

What should I check before I bid a fiber RFP?

Check that you have crews free in the window. Check that the codes fit your price book. Check the insurance limits, the as-built format, the closeout list and the payment terms. Note the bid due date and the questions deadline. If something is unclear, ask the customer before the questions deadline.

How are telecom procurement requests approved?

Each customer has its own path. Often the customer sets the scope, a team reviews the bids, and the customer picks one and makes the award. Then it may send a notice to proceed. That is the go to start work. Read the RFP to see how this one works, and ask if it is not clear.
