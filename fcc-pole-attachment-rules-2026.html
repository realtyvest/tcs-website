FCC Pole Attachment Rules Are Live. Here Is What Changes for Your Crews.

By Gil Ramirez August 12, 2026 5 min read

New FCC pole attachment rules took effect this month. If your crews are doing aerial work and waiting on utility pole access, the clock just changed on everyone involved, including the utilities.

The FCC overhauled its pole attachment rules to speed up broadband builds. The core idea is simple: utilities have been slow to process make-ready work, and that slowdown has been killing project timelines for years. The new rules put harder deadlines on utilities and create clearer dispute paths when those deadlines get missed.

Broadband Breakfast confirmed the rules are in effect. Exact deadline figures vary by pole owner and by state, so check the FCC docket for the numbers that apply to your corridors. What happens on the ground is the same either way. Utilities are now on a clock, and that changes what you can ask for.

Utilities Are Now on a Clock. That Is Your Leverage.

Before these rules, a utility could drag its feet on a pole survey or make-ready order with limited consequence. Your crew sat idle. Your ISP client pushed the schedule. You ate the cost.

Enforceable deadlines change that dynamic. When a utility misses a deadline set by an FCC rule, the attacher (in most cases the ISP you are working for) has grounds to escalate. That escalation can unlock self-help remedies. Self-help means the ISP can hire a qualified contractor to do the make-ready work itself and bill the utility.

Here is the call you have to make: do you want to be the contractor that gets that self-help call, or the one that finds out about it after the job is awarded?

What This Means for Your Pipeline and Your Crew Schedule

Faster pole access, if the rules hold, means shorter gaps between permit approval and construction start. That sounds like good news. It is, but it also compresses your scheduling window. The ISP that used to give you six weeks of advance notice because they were waiting on pole surveys may now give you three.

A 12-crew aerial contractor in the Southeast told us last year that pole access delays were their single biggest cause of crew downtime. Those crews burn payroll whether they are stringing fiber or waiting in a staging lot. Tighter utility timelines shrink that dead time. But only if your back office can move at the same speed. If your ISP sends a notice-to-proceed and your crew coordinator is still sorting last week's lien waivers, you lose the window.

Dispute Mechanisms Are Only Useful If You Know They Exist

The new rules reportedly strengthen the process for challenging utility delays. Your ISP client is the one with standing to file those challenges, not you directly. But you should know the process exists and push your ISP contacts to use it when a utility stalls a project.

Every week a pole survey sits unprocessed is a week your crews are not billing. That is real DSO impact and real working capital sitting still. You have every right to ask your ISP contact where the pole application stands and what they are doing to enforce the timeline.

The Three Things to Do This Week

  • Pull every active aerial project and list the current pole access status for each. Know which ones are waiting on utility action.
  • Talk to your ISP project manager contacts and ask specifically whether they are tracking utility deadlines under the new FCC rules.
  • If you have capacity to do make-ready work, make sure your ISP contacts know that. Self-help provisions create a new category of work. Be the contractor on the list before the opportunity surfaces.

The rules are in effect. The utilities know it. The ISPs know it. The contractors who move now will be better positioned when the log-jam starts to break.

Pole access delays are a cash flow problem dressed as a scheduling problem. Fix the access and the cash follows.
