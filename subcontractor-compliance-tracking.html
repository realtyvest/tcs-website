How to Track Contractor Compliance Before a Job Starts

By Gil Ramirez March 26, 2026 5 min read

One Non-Compliant Crew Can Put the Whole Job at Risk

An outside crew shows up to your fiber job. Crew of three. They've got the right skills. You've worked with them before. Your project manager dispatches them to the work site. They're there for three days, pulling fiber through conduit.

Six weeks later, the owner sends you a notice. One of the crew's certificates of insurance had lapsed while they were on site, and nobody caught it until closeout. Now the prime may hold your payment, depending on your subcontract, while it sorts out who covers that gap.

Your insurer may push back. The crew's insurer may say the lapse leaves that period uncovered. Meanwhile, the customer is asking whether to keep you on future work.

A document check before the crew walks on the job is how you avoid that conversation.

What Compliance Actually Looks Like

Compliance tracking isn't about being bureaucratic. It's about knowing exactly what your contractors have certified before they show up on your jobs. Different states, different customers, and different contract types require different documentation.

You're tracking these categories:

  • Certificate of Insurance (COI). Valid proof that the contractor has liability insurance. Not just that they have insurance once, but that it's current and valid on the day they show up on your job.
  • Workers Compensation Insurance (WC). Rules differ by state, and many primes require it in the subcontract even where the state does not. Check your state and each prime's requirements.
  • Certificates of coverage. Some states and public owners require a specific workers comp certificate for construction work. Ask the prime which form it needs.
  • Form W9. Gives you the contractor's taxpayer ID for 1099 reporting. Without it, you may have to withhold tax from payments. Ask your accountant.
  • Background checks and certifications. Depending on the customer and the work, you might need proof of certifications (fiber splicing, OSHA, etc.) or background checks.
  • Sanctions screening. Some government and large carrier contracts ask you to show that contractors have been checked against sanctions lists, such as OFAC's.

The pattern is the same: Each piece of documentation has an expiration date. You're not just collecting paperwork once. You're maintaining it. You're checking expiration dates. You're enforcing renewal before contractors start jobs.

The Domino Effect of One Missing Document

Let's say a contractor's COI is expired. You don't catch it before they show up. Here's what happens:

Day one. The crew is on the job. No one is tracking that the insurance is expired. They're doing the work. Production is happening. Everything looks normal.

Day three. Something happens. Minor injury. Property damage. Someone slips. Nothing catastrophic, but reportable. The incident gets logged.

Day 21. The incident is reported to the insurance company. The adjuster checks the contractor's coverage for that date. It's expired, and the claim may be denied.

Day 45. The owner may look to you, because the crew was on your job, and the prime may hold your payment, depending on your subcontract, until the coverage question is sorted out. Your own insurer may ask whether you checked the crew's coverage before dispatch.

Day 90. Your payment may still be on hold, the paperwork may still be going back and forth, and the customer may be rethinking future work. How it plays out depends on your state, your contracts, and your policies.

This is an example, not legal or insurance advice. Talk to your insurance agent and your attorney about your own coverage.

It doesn't take anything dramatic. One day with an uninsured crew and a minor incident can become your problem.

What a Tracking System Actually Does

You're not just collecting documents. You're keeping them current. TCS keeps each crew's and sub's COI, W9, DWC83 and certs in one place. The office checks there before assigning work.

Here's how it works:

  1. Upload initial documentation. When you start working with a new contractor, you collect their COI, W9, any certificate the prime requires, and any other required paperwork, and keep it all in one place.
  2. Documents with their dates. Keep each crew's COI, W9, any certificate the prime requires, and certs with their dates. The office can see which documents are expired or expiring before assigning the crew.
  3. Document turnover. When the contractor sends a renewed document, it goes in the same place. Check that the new dates pick up where the old ones end. You don't want contractors with coverage gaps.
  4. One place to check. The office checks the crew's documents and their dates in one place before assigning work.
  5. Customer audits. When a customer audits your compliance, you can pull each crew's documents and their dates from one place.

Different States, Different Rules

Requirements differ by state and by customer. If you work in more than one state, you are tracking more than one set of rules.

Take Texas as an example. Along with the COI and W9, a Texas prime may ask for DWC Form-083, the Texas Department of Insurance's joint agreement to affirm an independent relationship for certain building and construction workers.

Rules and forms vary by state, so check what your state and your customer require.

Manually tracking this across states is asking for trouble. You'll miss a requirement in one state and not realize it until a claim comes back.

The Compliance Tracking Workflow

Your crew assignments need to tie directly to your compliance documents. This is what that workflow looks like:

  1. You have a fiber job in Houston. You need three splicing techs for two weeks.
  2. You go to assign crews. The office can see which documents are expired or expiring before assigning the crew, and checks that the ones the customer and the job require are current for the job dates.
  3. You pick three crews whose documents are current through the end date of the job.
  4. The office assigns the three crews to the job.
  5. After the job closes, keep the documents and their dates in the same place in case a customer or insurer asks.

That's how you lower the risk, and how you show your work when a customer audits compliance.

What Happens Without a System

Without compliance tracking, you're relying on someone in your office to remember to check documents, chase renewals, and verify expiration dates before every assignment. That person is busy with 50 other tasks. They miss one renewal. One crew gets assigned with expired paperwork. One incident happens. You're exposed.

Keep each crew's and sub's documents in one place, and check them before you assign the work.

The Bottom Line

Compliance tracking isn't optional. It's risk management. One non-compliant crew on one job can leave you with a held payment, a long paperwork fight, and a lost customer. Keeping each crew's and sub's COI, W9, certs and any state forms your prime asks for (like Texas DWC Form-083) current, and checking them before the crew is assigned, is the difference between managed risk and unmanaged exposure.

You can't afford to miss this. Contractors renew their documents. Your jobs change. Your states change. Keep each crew's documents current, and check them before every assignment.
